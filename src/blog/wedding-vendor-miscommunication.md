Here's something couples don't hear often enough: on almost every wedding day where something goes wrong, the vendors themselves weren't the problem. Every one of them was excellent at their job. What broke was the **coordination between them.**

That distinction matters, because it changes what you should actually be worried about — and what actually protects your day.

## The invisible web

A modest wedding easily involves eight to twelve vendors: venue, caterer, photographer, videographer, DJ or band, florist, hair, makeup, officiant, transportation, rentals, sometimes a baker or a specialty vendor. Each of them shows up with their own timeline, their own load-in needs, their own assumptions about "how weddings usually work."

None of those assumptions match automatically. The caterer thinks setup starts at 2. The florist thinks the venue opens at 3. The photographer plans to shoot details "before guests arrive," which is another assumption. The DJ needs to sound-check *before* the ceremony rehearsal starts, not after. Nobody's wrong — they're each right about their own piece. But their pieces don't fit until someone actively fits them.

On a coordinated wedding, that's what a coordinator has been doing for weeks before the day. On a DIY wedding, most of the time, no one is.

## What actually goes wrong

A few honest, real-world examples of what "vendor miscommunication" looks like on a DIY wedding:

- The florist finishes the ceremony arch at 3:15 — ten minutes after the photographer needed it for the "details" shots. Those photos never get taken.
- The caterer arrives at the venue and can't find the coordinator (because there isn't one), so they set up where they think makes sense — which turns out to be the exact spot the DJ needs for their table.
- The hair stylist runs 20 minutes over. Nobody warns the photographer. The whole first-look window collapses.
- The officiant thinks the processional is timed to one song. The DJ thinks it's two. The bride starts walking to silence.
- The venue closes their kitchen at 9pm, but the cake cutting is scheduled for 9:30 because it was scheduled off the reception timeline, not the venue's operational timeline.

None of these are catastrophic on their own. Any one of them is a "story we laugh about later." A few of them stacked on top of each other, though, is what turns a wedding day from *magical* into *fine.*

## Why "we'll just handle it ourselves" isn't quite enough

Almost every DIY couple I've talked to said the same thing at the start: *we'll build a good timeline and email it to everyone, and it'll be fine.*

The problem isn't the timeline. The problem is that vendors don't always **read** the timeline (especially the parts about other vendors), don't always **remember** it under the pressure of a live event, and — most importantly — don't have a single point of contact when reality starts to drift from the plan. Which it will.

So on the day, when the caterer needs to know whether to hold service because ceremony started late, they text… who? The bride? The best man? Nobody? On a DIY wedding, it's usually "nobody," which means the caterer makes their best guess. Which might not match what the DJ is guessing. Which might not match what the photographer is guessing.

Multiply that by twelve vendors making twelve independent best-guesses, and you can feel why the day starts to fray.

## What a coordinator actually does between vendors

The real work of coordination — the part couples underestimate — is what happens in the two weeks *before* the wedding and the six hours *during* it:

- Every vendor is contacted individually, confirmed, and given the master timeline with the specific pieces that concern *them* highlighted.
- Load-in, setup, and breakdown windows are stacked so no one blocks anyone else.
- Every vendor gets a single phone number to text on the day (mine, not yours).
- When something drifts — and something always does — one person makes the call and everyone else gets updated in the same moment.

The couple, meanwhile, has no idea any of this happened. That's the whole point.

## The lowest-friction way to fix this

If you're planning most of your wedding yourself, you don't need to hire a full-service planner to solve the vendor-coordination problem. You need someone whose specific job is to become the single point of contact for all your vendors starting a few weeks out — that's exactly what a **day-of coordinator** does.

You keep the money you saved by DIY-ing everything else. You just protect the piece that DIY quietly fails at: the seamless communication between the twelve people who need to work in sync for your day to feel effortless.
