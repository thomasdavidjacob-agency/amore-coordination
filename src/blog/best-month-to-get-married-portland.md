Couples ask me this early, usually before they have a venue or a guest count: *what is the best month to get married in Portland?*

It is a fair question, and I will give you a straight answer: there is a best month for *weather*, and it is not necessarily the best month for *you*. The right choice depends on what you are willing to trade. This guide lays out what the climate record says, what each season actually means for planning, and how I would choose.

## What does the weather data say about Portland by month?

I only use one source for weather claims: the National Weather Service's [Climate of Portland](https://www.weather.gov/media/pqr/climate/ClimateBookPortland/PDXclimatebook.pdf) book, which uses 1991 to 2020 normals. Here is what it shows, in plain terms.

**Rain.** In the downtown normals, December is the wettest month, followed by November. July and August are the driest. The book also notes that rain varies across the metro area, and that the West Hills receive more than the airport does.

**Temperature.** In the airport normals, July and August have the highest average high temperatures. January and December have the lowest. The shoulder months, May and June in spring and September and early October in fall, sit between.

**Summer character.** The book describes summers as often dry and warm, with the occasional hot stretch that does not last, as cooler ocean air moves back inland. It also notes that May and June can still bring plenty of cloudy days, even though rain days drop off.

**Winter character.** It describes the winter season as mild, cloudy and rainy, with cold-air outbreaks from east of the Cascades that can occasionally bring freezing rain or snow, depending on the area.

A normal is an average over thirty years, not a forecast. Any single day can be different. I use the normals to understand the *pattern*, and I plan every wedding as if the day could surprise us.

## So is July or August the best month?

For the odds of dry weather, the numbers point there. But the driest months are also the most in demand, so they come with trade-offs:

- **Popularity.** Peak dates tend to book first, so you may have fewer venue and vendor options and less flexibility on the day of the week.
- **Heat.** The warmest months are not always the most comfortable for a ceremony in full sun, for guests in formalwear, or for an outdoor setup with no shade.
- **Competition for guests' time.** Summer is also when families travel and take time off, so check your key guests' calendars.

If a dry, warm day is your top priority, July and August are the logical place to start. Just go in knowing that you are not the only one who thinks so.

## What about June and September?

These are the months couples often mean when they say "best." They sit at the edges of the dry season, and many couples find that they combine reasonable weather with a bit more availability than the peak of summer.

**June** is still part of the spring-to-summer transition. The NWS book notes that even as rain days decrease in May and June, there are still plenty of cloudy days. Expect long daylight and a real chance of a gray morning.

**September** follows the driest stretch of the year and, in the downtown normals, is drier than the autumn months that come after. Light is softer and days are shorter than in summer, which matters for your timeline.

I would still plan a rain backup in either month. A good one is not pessimism. It is what lets you enjoy an outdoor ceremony without checking your phone. I wrote about exactly that in [planning an outdoor Oregon wedding with a rain backup](/blog/oregon-outdoor-wedding-rain-backup-plan/).

## What about spring and early fall?

**April and May** bring the blooms and the garden-ceremony feeling many couples want, along with changeable weather. Plan for layers, a covered option and flexibility on timing.

**October** is a favorite for its color and atmosphere. It is also when the rainy season is returning, and the downtown normals show it wetter than September. Daylight is getting shorter, which shapes when the ceremony and photographs should happen.

Neither is a bad choice. They are choices that reward a good plan.

## Are winter weddings worth considering?

Absolutely, if you plan for the season. A winter wedding can be warm, candlelit and cozy, and it often brings more flexibility in venue and vendor availability. It also comes with real constraints:

- **Rain and wet arrivals.** Plan covered entrances, a place for coats and umbrellas, and a plan for guests' shoes and hems.
- **Early darkness.** Your ceremony may need to be earlier than you expect, and the evening will be dark for much of the day.
- **Travel.** Winter weather can affect guests traveling, and on occasion a drive over the Cascades or through the Gorge. Give out-of-town guests clear information and some flexibility.
- **Indoor focus.** Choose a venue that is beautiful indoors, because that is where you will spend the day.

If winter is the season you want, it is worth a full plan rather than a rain backup. Cold and wet are not the exception. They are the setting.

## How does daylight change the answer?

Weather is only half of it. The length of the day decides when your ceremony can happen, how much time you have for photographs in natural light, and how long your guests are traveling in the dark.

- **In summer,** the long evenings give you flexibility. A later ceremony is possible, and the light stays pleasant for longer, though the sun can be strong at midday.
- **In spring and fall,** days are moderate and shifting. Build your timeline around when the light is best, and check it as your date approaches.
- **In winter,** daylight is short. If you want photographs outdoors, the ceremony usually needs to be earlier, and the evening will be dark for most of the party.

I plan the timeline for a wedding from the light backward, not from the venue's booking window.

## Does the day of the week matter as much as the month?

Often it does. A Friday or Sunday in a popular month can be easier to book than a Saturday, and some couples find that weekday weddings open up venue and vendor options. The trade-off is that guests have to travel and take time off, so think about who needs to be there and what that asks of them.

If your heart is set on a particular month, being flexible on the day of the week is one of the most useful things you can do. It is a conversation to have with a venue early, when you are comparing dates, not after you have fallen in love with one.

## What should I ask a venue about the month I want?

Whatever month you pick, ask the venue how that season looks in practice, because the answer is specific to the place:

- What does the space look like in this season, and what changes? Foliage, light, access and temperature can all be different.
- Where do we go if the weather is wet, hot or cold, and who decides?
- What are the arrival and parking arrangements in wet or dark conditions?
- What are the access hours, and how does daylight affect the schedule?
- What does the venue's calendar look like for the dates I am considering?

Write down the answers for every venue, so you can compare them fairly.

## How do I choose the month that is right for us?

Here is the order I walk couples through:

1. **What matters most?** Weather, availability, a particular atmosphere, or a specific guest's schedule. Pick the top one or two.
2. **How many guests are traveling?** More travel means more reason to consider the dry season, or to give extra notice.
3. **What kind of venue do you want?** An outdoor garden calls for different months than an indoor hall or a restaurant.
4. **What is the guest count?** Smaller weddings are often more flexible on date and season.
5. **How flexible is the day of the week?** A weekday or a Sunday in a popular month can be easier to book than a Saturday.
6. **How much lead time do you have?** If you are early, you can aim for a popular date. If you are late, choose a month where options are wider.

The question is less "which month is best?" than "which set of trade-offs do we prefer?"

## What if I cannot pick just one month?

Pick a window instead. Many couples do better choosing a range of acceptable dates across two or three months than waiting for a single perfect weekend. A window lets a venue offer you what is actually open, and it lets you choose on the quality of the venue and the vendors you love instead of on the calendar alone.

If you do this, rank your priorities before you start. A couple who values weather above all will choose differently from one who values a particular venue above all, and being clear about it makes every later decision faster. Then plan the same way for whichever month you land in: a real backup for rain or heat, a timeline built around the light, and clear information for guests who are traveling.

## A quick summary

- **Driest and warmest:** July and August, and in peak demand.
- **Balanced:** June and September, often a good compromise, with a rain plan.
- **Beautiful but changeable:** April, May and October.
- **Cozy and quiet:** November through February, with a plan built for rain, cold and early dark.

Choose the month that fits the day you want, then plan for the weather you might get. A good plan makes every month a good month.
