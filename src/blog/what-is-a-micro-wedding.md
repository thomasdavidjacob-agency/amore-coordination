Ask three people what a micro wedding is and you will get three answers. For some it means a few dozen guests. For others, a dinner for twenty. For others still, a ceremony with only immediate family.

That is not confusion. It is a sign that the term describes a *way of planning* more than a precise size. This guide explains what a micro wedding is, what actually changes when the guest list is small, what does not change, and how to decide whether it is the right shape for your day. I plan these weddings in the Portland area and Southwest Washington, so I will keep it practical.

## What is a micro wedding?

A micro wedding is a wedding with a deliberately small guest list, planned so that the day feels personal, unhurried and fully attended to.

There is no official definition. The size people mean varies, and you will see different numbers in different places. For my own work, I define it by what I offer: my [Micro Wedding package](/services/micro-wedding/) is designed for gatherings of up to 50 guests, and the details of the day are coordinated the same way they would be for a larger wedding.

What matters is the intent. A micro wedding is not a downgraded wedding. It is a wedding where the couple has chosen intimacy over scale, and then built the day around that choice.

## How is a micro wedding different from an elopement?

An elopement is usually built around the ceremony. It might be just the couple, or the couple and a handful of people, in a place that means something to them. I cover that in more detail in my guide on how to elope in Oregon, which is coming soon in this journal.

A micro wedding keeps more of the traditional structure: a guest list, a ceremony, a meal or reception, and a day with a shape. It is smaller, but it still has moving parts and vendors, and it still needs a timeline.

A simple way to tell them apart: an elopement centers on the moment; a micro wedding centers on the people in the room with you.

## What actually changes when the guest list is small?

Several things get easier and a few get more important.

**The venue options widen.** A small group fits places that a large wedding cannot: a restaurant's private room, a garden, a small hall, a home, a winery tasting space, a rented house. You are choosing for atmosphere instead of capacity.

**The budget can move.** With fewer guests, the same money can go toward different things: a better meal, a more beautiful setting, a photographer for longer, or simply keeping the total lower. I do not quote costs here, and I would not assume a small wedding is automatically cheaper. It is a different distribution.

**The guest list becomes the hard part.** In a large wedding, you can invite more people to avoid conflict. In a micro wedding, every name is a choice. The honest advice is to decide your principle before you decide your names.

**Details become visible.** Fewer guests means each one will notice the food, the seating, the music and the welcome. It is a good reason to invest in the details you care about.

**Every guest is an insider.** There is no back row. People will be part of the ceremony, the meal and the conversation. That can be lovely, and it means you should think about how guests will interact: seating, introductions, shared moments.

## What does not change?

Almost everything about logistics. A smaller guest list does not remove:

- **The marriage license.** A legal wedding requires one, whatever the size.
- **The timeline.** Ceremony, photographs, a meal, speeches and an end time all still need to be scheduled.
- **The vendors.** You may have fewer, but each one still needs to know when to arrive, where to set up and who to call.
- **The transitions.** Moving from ceremony to reception, from reception to dinner, from dinner to the end of the night.
- **The backup plan.** If any part of the day is outdoors, you still need a rain plan. My guide to [planning an outdoor Oregon wedding with a rain backup](/blog/oregon-outdoor-wedding-rain-backup-plan/) applies as much to 30 guests as to 150.

This is the point couples most often underestimate. A smaller wedding is not a simpler wedding. It is a wedding with fewer people to feed and more attention on every detail.

## How do I know if a micro wedding is right for us?

These questions tend to clarify it:

- **Who do we most want in the room?** If the answer is a short list and it feels right, that is a good sign.
- **Do we want the day to feel like a conversation or a production?** Micro weddings lean toward conversation.
- **Would we rather spend on the experience than on the headcount?** Many couples do.
- **Are we comfortable with the guest list conversations?** Telling people they are not invited is the hard part. It helps to settle your principle first.
- **Do we want a full wedding day, or only the ceremony?** If the ceremony alone is what you care about, an elopement may fit better.

None of these has a right answer. They help you notice what you actually want.

## How do I decide who makes the guest list?

This is the part couples dread, and it is easier with a principle than with a list. Pick a rule before you write a single name, and then apply it to everyone the same way. Some couples invite only people who have been part of their daily lives this past year. Some invite immediate family and the closest friends. Some invite only the people who would be at the table if the day were a dinner in their own home.

Whatever rule you choose, a few habits help:

- **Decide the ceiling first.** A number gives the rule something to push against.
- **Agree on the rule together,** and write it down, so it does not drift when someone asks for an exception.
- **Tell people early and kindly.** A short, warm explanation that the wedding is small is better than silence.
- **Plan a way to include people who are not there.** A casual get-together afterward, a photo, or a note works for many couples.

## What does a micro wedding day look like?

There is no template, which is part of the appeal, but a typical shape helps you see what needs planning. Here is one way a day can flow:

1. **Getting ready.** A quiet morning with a small group, with time built in for hair, makeup and attire.
2. **Arrival.** Guests are welcomed, seated and given a clear sense of what happens next.
3. **Ceremony.** Short, personal and heard by everyone in the room.
4. **Photographs.** Family and couple portraits, planned so guests are not waiting too long without a drink in hand.
5. **Meal.** Often the centerpiece of a micro wedding. A seated dinner, a long shared table or a restaurant buyout can feel very different from a standard reception.
6. **Toasts and moments.** A few, chosen on purpose.
7. **A graceful finish.** A clear end, an easy send-off and a ride home for anyone who needs one.

Each step needs a time, a person responsible and a plan for what happens if it runs late. That is the job a coordinator does, and it is the same job whether there are 20 guests or 120.

## Where can a micro wedding happen?

Almost anywhere that works for your group, which is a real advantage. I will not name specific venues here, because availability, rules and capacity change and the venue itself is the only reliable source. But the types of spaces couples consider include restaurants with private rooms, gardens and small outdoor venues, wineries, small halls and community spaces, rented homes and, with the right permissions, a backyard.

Whichever type you choose, ask the same questions:

- What is the real capacity for a seated meal with my guest count?
- What is included, and what do we need to bring or rent?
- What are the rules on food, alcohol, music and decor?
- When can vendors arrive, and when must we leave?
- What is the plan for weather, parking and accessibility?

## What does a micro wedding need to get planned?

The same sequence as any wedding, scaled to size. Here is the order I would follow:

1. **Decide the guest list principle and a ceiling.** The number shapes everything else.
2. **Choose the date and venue together.** Smaller venues often book up, so be ready to be flexible.
3. **Confirm the marriage license process.** Your county sets the rules. For example, Multnomah County explains on its [marriage license page](https://multco.us/services/get-marriage-license) how its process works, including how long you must wait after the license is issued and how long it stays valid. Other counties, including those in Southwest Washington, have their own rules, so check with the one where you are applying.
4. **Book the vendors that matter most.** Often a photographer, a caterer or restaurant, and an officiant.
5. **Build the timeline.** Even a short day needs one.
6. **Plan the guest experience.** Seating, welcome, travel and parking, and anything you want guests to remember.
7. **Confirm everything one last time.** Vendors, times, the venue and the license.

If it is helpful to see how timing works, my post on [when to book a wedding coordinator](/blog/when-to-book-a-wedding-coordinator/) has a realistic planning timeline that applies to smaller weddings too.

It depends on how much you want to carry yourselves. A micro wedding has fewer people, but it still has a day with a sequence: setup, ceremony, photographs, meal, speeches, a clean finish. Someone has to hold that, and if it is you, you are not fully there.

## What are common mistakes with micro weddings?

- **Assuming it needs no plan.** It needs a smaller plan, not no plan.
- **Deciding the guest list last.** It should be first.
- **Choosing a venue by looks alone.** Check access, parking, rules and what happens if it rains.
- **Skipping the license details.** Confirm the waiting period and validity with your county early.
- **Overpacking the day.** A small wedding feels best when it is not rushed.
