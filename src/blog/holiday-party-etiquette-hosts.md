Etiquette sounds formal, and it can seem like a topic for another century. But in practice, it is just a set of small kindnesses that help guests feel welcome and let the host stop worrying. When people say a party felt easy, they are usually describing good manners at work, even if they could not name them.

These are the habits I see make the biggest difference at holiday parties, in roughly the order they matter. They apply to a dinner for eight and to a reception for a hundred. If you want the full planning sequence, start with my [guide to holiday party planning in Portland](/blog/holiday-party-planning-portland-coordinators-guide/).

## What should the invitation say?

A good invitation answers every question a guest would otherwise have to ask. Include:

- **The occasion,** so guests know whether it is a celebration, a thank-you or a family gathering.
- **The day, the start time and the end time.** The end time is a kindness to everyone, especially people with a babysitter or a long drive.
- **The location,** with parking or transit notes if they matter.
- **What to wear.** A short phrase such as "festive casual" or "dressy" saves a lot of worry.
- **Whether food and drinks are served,** especially if it is a cocktail hour or a full dinner.
- **How and when to RSVP.** Give a clear reply-by date and one way to respond.
- **Who is invited.** Address the invitation to the specific people you mean.

Send invitations early. December calendars fill up, and an early note signals that you value the person's time.

## How do I handle RSVPs?

Ask for them, set a date and follow up. It may feel awkward to chase a reply, but a short, friendly message is part of hosting: "I am finalizing the headcount this week and wanted to check if you can join us."

A few habits help:

- **Track replies in one place.** A simple list with names, yes or no, dietary needs and plus-ones.
- **Expect a few changes.** People get sick, schedules shift. Keep a little flexibility in your numbers.
- **Reply to the replies.** A quick thank-you or "we will miss you" keeps things warm.

## What about plus-ones, children and guest lists?

Be clear and kind. If the invitation is for the person named on the envelope, say so by naming who is invited. If space is limited, you can explain that simply: "We are keeping the evening small this time, and we hope you understand."

You do not owe a long justification. You do owe a clear message early enough that people can plan. If children are welcome, say so, and if the evening is adults only, say so just as plainly. People can then decide what works for them.

## How do I welcome people when they arrive?

Greet guests at the door, or have someone do it for you. A personal welcome, a place for coats and a drink in hand within the first few minutes tells people they are expected.

Two small things that matter more than they seem:

- **Introduce people.** Take a moment to connect guests who do not know each other, with a line about what they have in common.
- **Point out the essentials.** Where the restrooms are, where to put coats and where the food is.

## How should I handle food and dietary needs?

Ask guests about dietary needs in the invitation or in a follow-up note. Then plan so that everyone has something satisfying to eat, not just a side dish.

- **Label the food.** A small card listing the dish and common allergens saves guests from asking.
- **Keep dishes separate where it matters.** Ask your caterer how they handle cross-contact.
- **Offer a real option for everyone.** A guest with dietary needs should not feel like an exception.

If a caterer or venue handles the food, ask them directly how they handle requests, and confirm in writing.

## What about drinks?

Offer good non-alcoholic choices alongside whatever else you serve, and treat them as part of the menu, not a backup. Guests choose not to drink for many reasons, and none of them should be noticed.

If you are serving alcohol, think about how guests will get home, and make that easy to talk about. The rules for serving alcohol depend on where you are hosting and who is serving, so ask your venue and caterer what they handle and what they expect from you. ## How should I handle toasts and speeches?

Keep them short, and decide in advance who is speaking. A host's welcome and thank-you can be a minute or two. Pause the music, make sure the person can be heard and thank people by name when it fits.

If several people want to speak, agree on the order beforehand, and put it on the timeline. For more on pacing the evening, see my post on [holiday party entertainment, music and activities](/blog/holiday-party-entertainment-music-activities/).

## What about gifts, tips and thank-yous?

If there is a gift exchange, set the rules in the invitation so no one arrives surprised or unprepared. If you work with vendors, ask about their policies on gratuity in advance and build it into your plan, instead of improvising at the end.

After the party, a short thank-you to guests, to anyone who helped and to your vendors is a graceful finish. It does not need to be elaborate. A message sent the next day is enough.

## How do I end the party gracefully?

Decide the end time ahead and put it on the invitation. As the time approaches, use gentle signals:

- A last call for drinks.
- Dessert or coffee as the final course.
- Music turned down, lights turned up.
- A short thank-you from the host.

Stand at the door when people leave, and thank them for coming. Guests remember how an evening ended almost as much as how it began.

## What if I am the host and I want to enjoy my own party?

That is the hardest part of etiquette, and the most overlooked. A host who is anxious, in the kitchen or checking the clock cannot greet anyone. So give yourself a plan:

- **Write the timeline** and share it with anyone helping.
- **Name a point person** for vendor questions.
- **Delegate setup and cleanup** so you are not doing both in a party outfit.
- **Decide your priorities.** If you can only do three things well, choose the welcome, the food and the goodbye.
