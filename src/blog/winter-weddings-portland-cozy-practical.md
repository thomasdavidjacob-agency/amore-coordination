I love a winter wedding. There is something about a room full of candlelight, warm layers and people who came in from the cold that makes a celebration feel close. And if you have your heart set on a particular venue, it is worth asking what winter dates they have open.

It is also a season that rewards a practical mind. Days are short, the weather does not cooperate on request and guests arrive wet. None of that is a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to plan it the way it deserves. This guide covers what I would think through, in order. If you are still choosing a season, start with my guide to [the best month to get married in Portland](/blog/best-month-to-get-married-portland/).

## What does the weather data say about a Portland winter?

I only use one source for weather claims: the National Weather Service's [Climate of Portland](https://www.weather.gov/media/pqr/climate/ClimateBookPortland/PDXclimatebook.pdf) book, which uses 1991 to 2020 normals. In plain terms:

- **Rain.** In the downtown normals, December is the wettest month, followed by November.
- **Temperature.** In the airport normals, January and December have the lowest average high temperatures.
- **Character.** The book describes the winter season as mild, cloudy and rainy, with cold-air outbreaks from east of the Cascades that can occasionally bring freezing rain or snow, depending on the area.

A normal is an average over thirty years, not a forecast. Any single day can be different. I use it to understand the pattern, and I plan every winter wedding as if the day could surprise us.

## What are the real advantages of a winter wedding?

Honestly, there are several, and they are worth naming:

- **Atmosphere.** Candles, evergreens, warm lighting and rich colors feel natural in the season, and you do not need much decor to set the mood.
- **Flexibility on dates.** Ask venues and vendors which winter dates they have open; you may find more choice of day than you expect.
- **An indoor, intimate feel.** If you like the idea of a gathering that feels close, winter does that on its own.
- **Seasonal food and drink.** Warm dishes, hot drinks and a menu built around the season are a treat.

## What are the trade-offs?

Also worth naming:

- **Short days.** Less daylight means less time for outdoor photographs and a tighter window for anything that depends on natural light.
- **Weather.** Rain is likely, and an occasional cold snap can change travel and plans.
- **Guest travel.** Holidays, family trips and travel that falls near your date may affect who can come.
- **Competing events.** Holiday gatherings and parties can crowd the calendar, especially around the holidays themselves.

Check your key guests' plans before you set a date, and give everyone plenty of notice.

## How do short days change the timeline?

This is the single biggest planning change in winter. Start with the daylight and work backward.

- **Choose your ceremony time with light in mind,** especially if you want photographs outside. An earlier ceremony gives you more usable light.
- **Plan portraits earlier.** Consider a first look or portraits before the ceremony, so you are not racing the dark afterward. Ask your photographer what they recommend.
- **Check sunset for your date.** It is easy to look up, and it decides when outdoor photographs have to be done.
- **Build in buffers.** Slow arrivals, wet weather and extra layers all add minutes.

The principle behind any good timeline is the one I describe in [the timeline trap in DIY weddings](/blog/diy-wedding-timeline-trap/): write it down, share it and leave room.

## Should I plan an outdoor ceremony in winter?

Possibly, with a real backup. If you love the idea, build the plan so that either version of the day works.

- **Decide who calls it and when.** A decision time, such as the morning of or a set number of hours before, keeps everyone from waiting.
- **Plan a covered or indoor option** that is already arranged and set, not improvised.
- **Keep the outdoor portion short.** A brief ceremony is easier on guests and on your timeline.
- **Tell your guests.** Let them know the plan, so they can dress for it.

I wrote more about this in my guide to [planning an outdoor Oregon wedding with a rain backup](/blog/oregon-outdoor-wedding-rain-backup-plan/), and nearly everything there applies in winter, with a bit more urgency.

## How do I keep guests comfortable?

Comfort is the difference between a cozy wedding and a long, cold one. Think about the whole route a guest takes.

- **Arrival.** A well-lit path, clear signs and a spot to shake out umbrellas.
- **Coats.** A coat check or a clearly marked area, so wet coats are not draped over chairs.
- **Warmth.** Check how the venue heats the space, and whether any outdoor area has heat or cover.
- **Walking distance.** Think about how far guests walk from parking in the rain, and how shoes and long dresses fare.
- **Warm drinks.** A hot drink station is as welcome as a toast.
- **Restrooms and seating.** Easy to find and comfortable.

Ask your venue what it offers and what you are responsible for. Do not assume that heat, coverage or coat space is included.

## What should I wear and what should I tell my guests to wear?

For yourself, think about layers and shoes as much as the outfit. A wrap, a jacket, warm tights or a second pair of shoes for the walk can keep you comfortable without changing the look.

For your guests, say a few words on your website or invitation about the setting: indoors or out, the walk from parking, and whether any part of the day is outside. People will dress to match what you tell them.

## What about decor, flowers and food?

Winter lends itself to texture: evergreens, candles, warm metals, rich fabrics and seasonal blooms. Ask your florist what is available in the season, and let them guide you.

For lighting, candles and warm lamps do more than almost any decor. Check the venue's rules on open flame, and ask what alternatives it allows.

Menus built around warm dishes feel right, and guests will thank you for hot food on a wet day. If you want a small wedding with a thoughtful meal, take a look at [what a micro wedding is](/blog/what-is-a-micro-wedding/).

## What do I do about vendor timing and loading in?

In winter, vendors arrive in rain, dark and sometimes traffic. Give them more time than you think, and give each a written arrival time, a place to park and unload, and a point of contact. Confirm details a few days ahead. Most of what I do before a wedding is preventing small delays from becoming big ones.

## A short winter wedding checklist

- Choose a date with your key guests in mind.
- Look up sunset for the date, and build the timeline around the light.
- Decide on an indoor or covered plan, and who calls it.
- Ask the venue about heat, coat space and rules on candles.
- Tell guests about the setting and the walk.
- Give every vendor a written arrival time and a contact.
- Pack for the day: umbrellas, towels, extra layers and a spare pair of shoes.

Plan for the weather you are likely to get, enjoy the weather you actually get, and let the season do some of the decorating for you.
