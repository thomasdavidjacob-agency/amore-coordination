Ask a host what they are worried about for a holiday party, and entertainment usually comes up. What will people do? Is there enough going on? Will it be awkward if there is not?

Here is what I have learned from coordinating events: guests rarely remember the activity list. They remember whether they felt welcome, whether they could talk to the people they came to see, and whether the evening had a shape. Entertainment should serve that, not compete with it. This is how I think about choosing it. For the bigger picture, my [guide to holiday party planning in Portland](/blog/holiday-party-planning-portland-coordinators-guide/) walks through the whole sequence.

## How much entertainment does a holiday party really need?

Start with the purpose of the party. A team thank-you, a client appreciation evening and a family gathering want different things.

- **A thank-you party** wants a warm welcome, good food and a few sincere words.
- **A client event** wants easy conversation and a setting that makes introductions natural.
- **A family or friends party** wants room to talk, something to do with kids and maybe one shared tradition.

In each case, the foundation is the same: a comfortable room, good food on time, a clear start and end, and one moment worth remembering. Everything else is optional. If you are pressed for time, my post on [last-minute holiday party planning](/blog/last-minute-holiday-party-planning/) explains what to protect and what to let go.

## How do I choose music?

Music sets the temperature of the room, so think about what you want people to do.

- **Arrival and mingling.** Softer, familiar music at a volume where people can talk without leaning in.
- **Dinner.** Quieter still. Guests at a table are in conversation.
- **A toast or program.** Pause the music well ahead, and make sure the person speaking can be heard.
- **Dancing, if you want it.** Give people a signal that it is time, such as moving the main event to a new area or changing the lighting.

Your options, in rough order of complexity:

1. **A playlist and a speaker.** Simple, flexible and enough for many small parties. Build it ahead, and keep a backup you can switch to if the first fails.
2. **A solo or small live musician.** Lovely for atmosphere, especially during a reception or dinner. Ask about their setup, how much space they need and when they can arrive.
3. **A DJ or band.** The best choice for a dancing party, and the one that needs the most coordination.

Whichever you choose, ask your venue what it allows for amplified sound, power and equipment, and when entertainers can load in. Confirm those answers with the venue before you book anyone, since policies differ from place to place.

One practical point: a playlist built for your own taste may not suit a mixed group. Choose music that is familiar and friendly, and skip anything that needs to be explained.

## What activities work at a holiday party?

Think of activities as invitations, not assignments. They give guests something to do with their hands and a reason to talk to someone new, and they should never be mandatory.

Ideas that tend to work:

- **A photo spot.** A simple backdrop, good lighting and a place where guests can pose together. Mention it, then let people find it.
- **A tasting or a station.** Hot drinks, a dessert table or a build-your-own station gives people a place to gather and something to talk about.
- **A card or note table.** Guests write a short thank-you or a good wish, either for the host or for each other.
- **A gift exchange.** Fun for a group that knows each other. Set the rules in the invitation so nobody is surprised.
- **Games and cards,** if the group is small and comfortable.
- **A simple tradition.** A toast, a reading, a song or a short round of thank-yous that fits your group.

Ideas I would approach with caution:

- **Anything that puts guests on the spot.** Public speaking, forced dancing and embarrassing games are better as volunteer-only.
- **Anything with a long setup.** If it needs more than a few minutes to explain, it will eat the evening.
- **Too many at once.** Two or three options are plenty.

## What should I plan for children and mixed ages?

If guests of different ages are coming, plan a little for each group. Children usually need something that is theirs: a table with simple crafts, a quiet corner or a short activity with a clear end. Older guests often appreciate comfortable seating near the action, but not directly beside the speakers.

## How do I pace the evening?

A party works best when it has a shape: a beginning, a middle and an end. A rough shape for many parties:

1. **Arrival.** Greet people, take coats and get a drink in every hand.
2. **Settling in.** Light food and mingling while everyone arrives.
3. **The main moment.** A toast, a thank-you or a short program, kept to a few minutes.
4. **The meal or main food.** Served on time.
5. **Winding up.** Dessert, music, a final round of conversation and a clear last call.

Write your timeline backward from the end, and share it with anyone who has a role, including the venue, the caterer and any entertainer. Everyone should know when their part begins and when it ends.

## How long should a program be?

Shorter than you think. A toast of a minute or two lands better than a speech that tests the room. If several people want to speak, ask them to keep it brief and agree on the order beforehand. And pick one place in the evening for it, not several.

## What can go wrong, and how do I prepare?

- **The equipment fails.** Test everything ahead, and keep a backup playlist or a second speaker.
- **The volume is wrong.** Check it with the room full, not empty. A full room absorbs sound.
- **The entertainer arrives late or early.** Put arrival times in writing, and agree where they park and unload.
- **No one is in charge of the moment.** Name the person who starts the program, cues the music and brings people together.
- **The schedule runs long.** Decide in advance what you will cut first.
