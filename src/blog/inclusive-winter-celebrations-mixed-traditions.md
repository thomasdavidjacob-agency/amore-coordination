When a group includes people with different backgrounds, beliefs and family traditions, a holiday party raises a good question: how do we gather in a way that feels welcoming to everyone?

I do not have a single answer, and I will not pretend that one approach fits every group. What I can offer is a planning approach. It is built on a simple idea: ask people, plan carefully and communicate kindly. I am a coordinator, not an authority on anyone's tradition, so this guide stays with the practical side of planning and leaves the traditions themselves to the people who observe them. For the general sequence of planning any party, start with my [guide to holiday party planning in Portland](/blog/holiday-party-planning-portland-coordinators-guide/).

## What does an inclusive celebration mean?

To me, it means that every guest can come, eat, join in or step back without feeling singled out or having to explain themselves. It does not mean removing everything that gives a party its character. It means making intentional choices, so no one feels like an afterthought.

That usually comes down to five things: when you hold it, what you call it, what you serve, how you decorate and how you communicate.

## How do I choose a date and time?

Check the calendar before you commit. Different traditions observe different days at different times of the year, and some are not tied to a fixed date. If your guest list is varied, a quick look at key dates for the people you are inviting can save a conflict later.

A few habits help:

- **Ask early.** A friendly note, such as "We are thinking of gathering in December. Are there days that are difficult for you?", does more than guessing.
- **Offer options.** Two or three possible dates let people say which works.
- **Give notice.** December calendars fill up, so earlier is kinder.
- **Consider a different shape.** A daytime gathering, a weeknight or an early-January party after the busiest stretch can suit a mixed group well.
- **Do not pressure.** If someone cannot come, accept it warmly.

If you are short on time, my post on [last-minute holiday party planning](/blog/last-minute-holiday-party-planning/) explains how to simplify without losing what matters.

## What should I call the party?

Use words that fit your group. Many hosts choose something general, like "winter celebration," "end-of-year gathering" or "friends and family dinner," and let the invitation describe the evening. Others name a specific tradition because they are hosting one, and that is fine too, as long as guests know what to expect.

The principle is clarity. Tell people what the event is, what will happen and whether there is anything they are expected to take part in. Then no one has to guess.

## How should I handle food?

Food is where inclusion becomes concrete, so plan it with care.

- **Ask in the invitation.** Include a line asking about dietary needs, allergies and preferences, with a reply-by date.
- **Do not assume.** Needs and preferences vary within every group, and the people best placed to tell you are your guests.
- **Label every dish.** A card with the dish name and main ingredients, including common allergens, saves guests from asking.
- **Talk to your caterer.** Ask how they handle requests, ingredients, preparation and serving, and put the answers in writing.
- **Offer real choices.** A guest with specific needs should have a satisfying meal, not just a side dish.
- **Think about serving.** Separate serving utensils for each dish, and place dishes so guests can see what is what.
- **Offer good non-alcoholic drinks.** Treat them as part of the menu.

## How do I decorate for a mixed group?

Neutral and seasonal usually works best. Candles, warm lighting, evergreens, natural textures, winter colors and simple tables suit many groups without pointing to one tradition.

A few ideas:

- **Let the season lead.** Winter itself offers plenty: lights, greenery, warm tones.
- **Keep any specific symbols intentional.** If you include something tied to a tradition, do it because a guest or family wants it, and ask them how they would like it handled.
- **Avoid assumptions.** Not every guest celebrates in the way a standard party theme assumes.
- **Make decor optional.** A table with a few well-chosen details feels calm and welcoming.

## What about music, activities and the program?

Keep it friendly and optional. Familiar background music, a playlist that avoids anything that needs explaining and activities that invite but do not require are a good formula. For ideas, see my post on [holiday party entertainment, music and activities](/blog/holiday-party-entertainment-music-activities/).

If you plan a toast or a short program, keep it brief and warm, and think about whether the words fit everyone in the room. A simple thank-you for the people who came is almost always welcome.

## How do I communicate with guests?

Plan the message as carefully as the party.

- **Say what the evening will be.** The occasion, the timing, the food and any planned activities.
- **Invite questions.** A line such as "Let me know if there is anything that would make the evening easier for you" opens the door.
- **Be specific about what is optional.** If there is a gift exchange or a group activity, say that it is optional.
- **Respond warmly.** If someone raises a concern, thank them. They are helping you host well.
- **Follow up.** A short message afterward thanking people for coming is a good habit.

## What about children, family and workplaces?

The same principles apply, with a few adjustments.

- **Families.** Ask each household what they need, and keep children's food and activities simple and clearly labeled.
- **Workplaces.** A mixed team calls for a welcoming, optional and low-pressure gathering. Make attendance voluntary, choose a time that works for many schedules and keep the program light.
- **Friends and community groups.** Ask a few people from different backgrounds what would make the evening comfortable, and listen.

## What can go wrong, and how do I prepare?

- **A guest feels left out.** Ask before the event, and make it easy to say so afterward.
- **Food labeling is missing or wrong.** Confirm with the caterer, and label everything before guests arrive.
- **A date conflicts for someone.** Check early, and offer alternatives where you can.
- **A well-meant choice lands badly.** Apologize simply, fix what you can and move on.

No plan removes every risk. A host who has thought about it, asked and cared is usually forgiven the rest.
