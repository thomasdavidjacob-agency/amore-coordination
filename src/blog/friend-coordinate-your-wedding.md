I've had this exact conversation with more couples than I can count. They tell me their budget is tight, so they're going to ask their most organized friend — the sister with the color-coded spreadsheets, the college roommate who ran the sorority events, the cousin who's *"basically already a planner anyway."*

I always ask the same gentle question back: *have you told her yet what you'd actually be asking her to give up?*

Because the answer is usually more than the couple realized, and more than the friend realized when she said yes.

## What you're actually asking

When you ask a friend to "just coordinate" your wedding, here's the real ask, translated:

*Please arrive at 8am on my wedding day. Skip getting ready with the girls. Skip the mimosa. Don't sit down to eat when everyone else does. Answer every vendor question. Manage the timeline. Deal with the family drama if it comes up. Field the calls from the caterer, the DJ, the officiant, and the photographer. Be the last to leave. And please do all of this while I'm the one everyone wants to hug and celebrate, so I can't help you if it gets hard.*

Almost no friend, however capable, fully pictures that when they say yes. They're picturing "helping out." What actually shows up on the day is a **twelve-hour unpaid job during someone else's celebration.**

## The friendship cost

Here's the part no one talks about. When a friend coordinates your wedding, one of two things almost always happens:

**Either something goes wrong** — which is inevitable, because things always go wrong at weddings — and now it's *your friend's fault* in a way it would never be a hired professional's fault. That story becomes a scar tissue in the friendship neither of you can fully see around.

**Or nothing goes wrong**, but your friend didn't actually get to be at your wedding. She didn't dance. She didn't cry during your vows because she was watching for the officiant's cue. She wasn't in the photos because she was directing the photographer to where you were. She was working. And she'll smile and say "of course, I was happy to," but something small will have been taken from her that she can't quite name.

Either way, you paid a price the invoice didn't show.

## The wedding-day cost

Beyond friendship, there's the operational reality. A friend, no matter how organized, is doing this **for the first time.** She hasn't seen 230 weddings; she's seen a few she attended. She doesn't know that Skamania Lodge's kitchen is on the opposite side of the venue from what looks natural, or that Hoyt Arboretum's parking situation forces you to build in an extra 20 minutes for guest arrival. She doesn't have vendor relationships to smooth over conflicts. She can't quietly call a photographer she's worked with 40 times and shift the timeline in real time.

None of this is her fault. It's just that "willing" and "experienced" are very different things, and only one of them protects your day when reality starts to drift.

## When a friend genuinely CAN help

I don't want to make it sound like friends and family have no role. They absolutely do. The trick is giving them a **specific, contained job that doesn't require them to be in charge.** For example:

- **Point person for a single vendor or category** ("Auntie is in charge of making sure grandma is comfortable and has everything she needs.")
- **Personal-item runner** — carrying rings, escort cards, the vow booklets, the emergency kit.
- **Timeline reader-outer at the rehearsal** — pointing out where each person stands.
- **The "greeter"** for guests arriving early.

Small, defined, joyful jobs. Not "run my wedding."

It's the one hire specifically designed for couples who want to keep the planning (and the savings) but not lose their day, or their friendships, to running the event themselves.
